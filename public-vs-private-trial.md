# The Public Trial and the Private Trial

## Core framing

**The public trial:** Did George Ives murder Nicholas Tiebolt?

**The private trial of the system itself:** Can we possibly do this again?

## Public

The town stages civilization in the literal sense. With no courthouse, it builds one from a wagon-bench, a fire, a wooden box, a jury, witnesses, argument, and procedure. The trial is performative, but it is not fake. Everyone participates in proving that the forms of law matter.

## Private

The same process exposes every participant. An acquitted Ives goes free in the eyes of the law, but so do the witnesses, jurors, judge, and lawyers. They all return to the same few settlements. No police force, secure jail, protected courthouse, or state apparatus stands between them and retaliation.

The private question is not whether an acquittal is legally valid. It is whether this community can survive the answer “not guilty”—and whether it can expose itself to the same danger for every suspected road agent.

## Two sides of the same coin

Do not reduce the history to a simple sequence:

> Trial → guilty verdict → Vigilance Committee

The stronger structure is simultaneity. The public process moves toward a verdict on Ives while the private process moves toward a verdict on due process itself. The guilty verdict is convenient, but it does not settle the larger question. Trial and vigilantism are competing systems born from the same crisis, not merely cause and consequence.

Historical caution: the record supports private fear and discussion during the trial more firmly than it proves that a formally constituted committee had already reached a final decision before the verdict. Preserve that distinction by dramatizing a conclusion hardening among individuals before formal organization.

“Outside the courtroom, men may already have decided that the next George Ives will never get this far.”
