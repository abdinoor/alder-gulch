# Cast

This is the working cast architecture for the complete book. Inclusion here preserves research and narrative possibilities; it does not establish every reported detail as canon or commit every candidate to the manuscript. Character-specific current states and publication canon belong in `canon/characters/` after a person is selected for active use.

## Principal through-line characters

These characters carry a major line of continuity across multiple parts of the book. This designation concerns narrative architecture, not moral centrality or historical importance.

### Captain James Williams — evidence, doubt, and operational force

- **Status:** Selected principal through-line character.
- **Introduction:** During the investigation of Nicholas Tiebolt's death, subject to verification of Williams's precise involvement and firsthand knowledge.
- **Initial position:** Williams confronts the physical consequences of the killing. His revulsion gives the demand for decisive action an evidentiary and moral foundation and initially aligns the reader with the desire to eradicate the road agents.
- **Trial movement:** The Ives trial separates what Williams directly knows from what testimony, reputation, association, and inference suggest. The defense can introduce legitimate doubt without persuading him that no atrocity occurred. Williams may resent being influenced by uncertainty while recognizing that the court must make distinctions he would prefer to collapse.
- **Turn:** Doubt does not necessarily weaken his commitment. It helps convince him that adjudicating one provable crime at a time cannot protect the community from people he believes are dangerous.
- **Vigilante movement:** Williams becomes an operational leader, converting collective judgment into companies, pursuit, capture, and execution.
- **Long arc:** He begins close to unusually strong physical evidence and helps build a system that eventually acts on confessions, associations, reputations, and allegations of inconsistent quality. His movement connects the public question of provable guilt to the private turn toward preventive security.
- **Reader relationship:** Williams is a reader-alignment character, not an artificial reader surrogate. The reader can initially share the conclusion his experience makes persuasive, then perceive the widening gap between the quality of evidence in the Tiebolt case and the evidence used in later actions.
- **End-state question:** Determine whether Williams ever recognizes that widening gap, suppresses it, regards it as necessary, or remains convinced that the results vindicate the method. Do not resolve this without evidence or author decision.
- **Evidence constraints:** Verify the source of the title “Captain”; his role in finding or examining Tiebolt; the physical evidence he personally observed; his arrest, investigation, or trial participation; the timing and scope of his Vigilante command; and any surviving statements that can support interior treatment. Lew Callaway's later, family-connected account may magnify Williams's role.

## Principal-status decisions still open

- George Ives is central to the accusation and trial, but his status as a principal through-line beyond his execution has not been defined.
- Wilbur Fisk Sanders is a likely long-range institutional counterpart to Williams, but his precise arc and principal status remain to be decided.
- Henry Plummer is essential to the book's later conflict, but importance alone does not determine whether he carries a principal through-line.
- Nicholas Tiebolt is foundational to the story and must retain human presence after death, but he cannot provide a continuous active line through the later parts.

## Selected or provisionally selected supporting cast

### Henry Edgar — discovery and transformation

- **Status:** Selected as Chapter II's opening focal character.
- **Part I function:** Opens Chapter II with the May 1863 discovery and carries the thread from Chapter I's description of the strike into the lived discovery, attempted secrecy, rush from Bannack, division of claims, and rapid transformation of Alder Gulch. The chapter then advances through the settlement's growth to the December 1863 narrative present.
- **Source opportunity:** Edgar's diary may anchor details in a participant's record rather than retrospective scenic invention.
- **Dramatic value:** He can leave an unremarkable landscape and return with a crowd whose arrival makes the former landscape impossible.
- **Constraints/questions:** Identify exact diary entries, their dates, publication/transcription history, and what Edgar personally witnessed before drafting his interior experience.

### The Sheehan family — domestic economy and emerging community

- **Status:** Selected as the leading family through which Chapter II may show women, children, commerce, mobility, and vulnerability without reducing them to peril.
- **Household:** Freighter James Sheehan; his wife Anne Cleary Sheehan; his daughter Mary “Mollie” Sheehan, about ten in 1863; baby Kate; and niece Ellen Sheehan.
- **Mary's source:** Mary began dictating her reminiscences to her daughter Margaret Ronan in 1929. Margaret transcribed and organized them as a 1932 master's thesis. The modern Montana Historical Society edition was edited by Ellen Baumler and rearranges some material for chronology and clarity.
- **Reported experience:** The family reached the Bannack/Alder Gulch rush in 1863; James worked as a freighter; the household lived in a large Wallace Street cabin and began taking boarders because freight supplies allowed Anne and Ellen to set a good table. Mary remembered Henry Edgar and other discovery men boarding there, with Jack Gallagher and George Ives sometimes taking meals.
- **Mary's activity:** She remembered staking an imaginary claim, attending Thomas Dimsdale's school, roaming with Carrie Crane, gathering flowers and edible greens for sale, and cleaning miners' sluice boxes.
- **Dramatic value:** Anne helps show domestic labor becoming part of the instant economy. Mary supplies curiosity, mobility, enterprise, and a child's partial understanding of adult danger. James connects household survival to freight roads and portable gold.
- **Evidence boundary:** Most intimate detail is Mary's firsthand but decades-later recollection. The story that Ives spared James because his wife and daughters lived in the gulch is nested hearsay and must not be narrated as established fact. Anne's actions are better evidenced than her interior life; motives and opinions require further evidence or explicit reconstruction.

### Thomas Josiah Dimsdale — education and first history

- **Status:** Selected over Langford for possible Chapter II introduction.
- **Background:** Educated English immigrant who reached Virginia City in summer 1863, reportedly seeking relief from tuberculosis. He operated a private school and singing school in the winter of 1863–64, later became territorial superintendent of public instruction and editor of the *Montana Post*, and died in Virginia City in 1866 at about thirty-five.
- **Connection:** Mary Sheehan remembered attending his school and described him as small, delicate-looking, gentle, and frequently writing at his makeshift desk.
- **Later function:** His newspaper series became *The Vigilantes of Montana* (1866), the first major published account of the movement and reportedly the first book printed in Montana.
- **Dramatic value:** Connects families and education taking root to the later construction of vigilante history: he first teaches children, then teaches later readers how to understand the Vigilantes.
- **Evidence boundary:** Dimsdale wrote close to the events but explicitly intended to prove the necessity and equity of vigilante action. Do not foreshadow him as if he already knows the later story in Chapter II.

### Nathaniel Pitt Langford — retrospective enlargement

- **Status:** Retained but deferred, perhaps until Part V.
- **Background:** New York-born banker and businessman who joined the 1862 Fisk overland expedition, became involved in Montana commerce and the vigilance movement, served as a federal revenue collector and bank examiner, joined the 1870 Yellowstone expedition, and became Yellowstone's first superintendent.
- **Later function:** Published *Vigilante Days and Ways* roughly three decades after the events.
- **Dramatic value:** Represents the established participant who preserves and enlarges the heroic history for later generations.
- **Evidence boundary:** Valuable participant history shaped by elapsed time, reputation, and strong pro-vigilante commitments. Do not duplicate Dimsdale's Chapter II function.

## Essential roles still requiring selection or verification

### Henry Plummer — formal office and disputed criminality

- Sheriff and essential long-term character.
- Embodies the uncertainty of an office that exists without sufficient institutional force and the disputed claim that its holder led an organized road-agent band.
- Full introduction is currently reserved for Chapter VII; earlier mentions should preserve the dispute.

### Wilbur Fisk Sanders — law and legitimacy

- Prosecutor in the Ives trial and principal bridge between adversarial legal process and the Vigilante solution.
- Must not become an uncomplicated frontier hero; ambition, courage, anger, legal principle, and motivated belief may coexist.
- Likely introduced late in Part I or at the threshold of the trial.

### Paris S. Pfouts — commerce and civic decision

- Merchant/businessman traditionally identified as president of the Vigilance Committee.
- Strong possible Chapter II inhabitant because he can connect commerce, property, civic standing, and later extralegal organization.
- Verify his arrival, business activity, location, and presence during the specific Chapter II period before selecting him as a focal participant.

### Dr. Don Byam — improvised judicial procedure

- Acted as judge in the Ives trial according to project background.
- Best introduced when the community actually needs a judge, probably Chapters VIII–XI, rather than loaded into Chapter II.
- Verify biography, local standing, selection, authority, and conduct of the proceeding.

### Principal defense lawyer or lawyers

- Essential function: make evidentiary weakness and reasonable doubt legitimate, proving the proceeding is not merely a show trial.
- Identify counsel, divide their roles, and determine whether one deserves a recurring arc before naming a principal defender.

### Central evidentiary witness or witnesses

- Essential function: connect Tiebolt's disappearance, physical evidence, Ives, and the defense's challenges.
- Research the witness sequence, personal relationships, knowledge limits, contradictions, and later retellings before promotion to the recurring cast.

### Person close to Tiebolt

- Essential function: preserve Tiebolt as a person rather than merely the cause of Ives's trial.
- Butschy, Clark, Dempsey, an employer, associate, finder, or another documented connection may fill this role.
- Verify the relationships; do not invent intimacy from an economic association.

### Discoverer of Tiebolt or first alarm-raiser

- Essential function: turns private disappearance and injury/death into public fact.
- Determine who found Tiebolt, in what condition, who heard his statements if any, and how word reached the settlements.

### One juror, if records support it

- Potentially gives human scale to the burden placed on the jury.
- Use only if a juror can be identified and supported beyond a name; otherwise keep the jury collective.

### Indigenous presence

- The pre-strike landscape was not an empty human world merely because it lacked a settler town.
- Research historically specific Indigenous use, presence, displacement, and settler encounters relevant to Alder Gulch in 1863.
- Do not add an Indigenous character solely to correct settler perspective without evidence, agency, and a defensible narrative place.

## Secondary or limited-use candidates

### John Xavier “X” Beidler — enforcement

- **Status:** Selected as a sharp secondary presence, introduced at the Ives trial and used selectively when impatience with procedure hardens into lethal action. Williams carries the larger operational arc.
- **Background:** Pennsylvania-born worker and frontiersman variously employed as shoemaker, clerk, prospector, pack-train operator, freighter, stage guard, Vigilante, and deputy U.S. marshal.
- **Trial connection:** Later accounts attribute to him the famous interruption of Ives's request for delay: “Sanders, ask him how long he gave the Dutchman.” Verify wording and provenance before use.
- **Dramatic value:** Embodies impatience with procedural delay and the immediate enforcement of fatal consequences. His later formal law-enforcement service complicates the border between law and vigilantism.
- **Use constraint:** Do not make him the face of the entire movement; his vividness must not conceal the responsibility of civic organizers, commanders, and the wider supporting population.
- **Risk:** His vivid persona can turn collective civic responsibility into the story of one colorful frontier tough.

### Buck Stinson and Ned Ray — alleged associates

- Useful to suggest Ives's associations and later connect the campaign to Plummer.
- Keep peripheral in Part I unless a specific robbery, witness, or documented interaction requires more.
- Do not let a growing roster make the alleged road-agent organization appear proven.

### Additional alleged road agents

- Introduce only when a particular act, testimony, confession, arrest, or pursuit requires them.
- Maintain distinctions among documented association, accusation, confession, disputed organization, and proven conduct.

## Functions not requiring separate characters

### Reader surrogate

- Do not create or elevate a person solely to ask modern readers' questions.
- Henry Edgar, Mary Sheehan, a newcomer, a merchant, a witness, or a juror may orient the reader naturally from within a documented life.

### Generic “women and children”

- Do not use as an atmospheric unit whose only purpose is vulnerability.
- The Sheehans are the leading specific family; other women and children should appear through documented work, desire, conflict, pleasure, and community participation.

### Interchangeable Vigilante boosters

- Avoid a crowd of men making the same argument. Preserve distinct functions: Sanders supplies legal legitimacy; Pfouts civic decision; Williams organized force; Beidler immediate enforcement; Dimsdale and Langford shape historical memory.

## Provisional Part I introduction sequence

- **Chapter II — The Gulch:** Henry Edgar; the Sheehan family; Thomas Dimsdale. Pfouts only if chronology and scene purpose justify him.
- **Chapter III — The Roads:** Plummer at the edge of the crisis; selected travelers/freighters; at most one or two alleged associates.
- **Chapter IV — The Body:** finder or alarm-raiser; a documented person close to Tiebolt; early investigators including Williams if verified.
- **Chapters V–VI — Ives / accusation:** central witnesses, Ives's defenders and associates, and competing interpretations of evidence.
- **Chapters VII–VIII — Plummer / arrest:** full Plummer introduction; Sanders, Byam, principal defense counsel, and possibly a juror or civic organizer.
- **Part V or later:** Langford as participant-historian and enlarger of the inherited story.
