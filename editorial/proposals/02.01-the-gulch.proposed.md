# Chapter Two

## The Gulch

<!-- beat: consequential-pan -->

Alder Gulch began with a pan of dirt and a secret six men could not keep.

In May 1863, it did not look like much of anything men would cross a continent to possess. A creek ran northwest between low slopes toward the valley of the Stinking Water. Alder and willow crowded portions of its banks. Above them rose the bare shoulders of the Tobacco Root Mountains. There were no streets, no stores, no names fixed to boards. Whatever human histories already belonged to the gulch had not approached the particular vision of ownership the prospectors carried with them.

Henry Edgar came to it as one of six tired men returning to Bannack. They had left in February to look for gold farther east. The expedition had failed before the Crow turned them back from the country they had entered. They had been hungry, lost, pursued, and uncertain of their way. Now familiar waters lay ahead of them. Bannack meant food, shelter, and men they knew. The prospecting could have ended there without dishonor. Most expeditions ended with less than they had hoped to find.

The party stopped beside the creek on May 26. Accounts would later differ over the first pan. In one, William Fairweather noticed exposed rimrock while looking after the horses. In another, the men wanted enough gold to buy tobacco when they reached Bannack. The amount recovered was remembered as thirty cents, a dollar and seventy-five cents, two dollars and forty cents, and nearly an ounce from several pans. The yield improved or diminished according to the memory of the teller.

It did not matter. The dirt carried gold. Fairweather and Edgar washed another pan. Then another. Before the party left, the men had found gold at several points along the creek. They marked claims for themselves. Where the water slowed around bends or boulders, they gave names to the stream bars that had possessed none the day before.

The discovery transformed the ground first in their own minds. A bank became a claim. Distance became protection. Secrecy became value. They agreed not to tell anyone in Bannack what they had found. Then they rode toward the nearest collection of people and supplies.

<!-- beat: news-outruns-secrecy -->

Gold was almost impossible to keep secret in a mining camp because every man was waiting for someone else to find it.

The men who recently struck pay dirt needed provisions, tools, and animals enough to work what they had marked. Their return produced questions. Men noticed purchases. They watched preparations. Stories began before facts were available to restrain them. Edgar later remembered strangers in a saloon already saying the party had brought in a horse-load of gold. The discoverers attempted to leave Bannack without company. Men followed them anyway.

By the time concealment became impossible, two or three hundred miners were prepared to go. The party accepted the crowd on the promise that its first claims would be respected. The agreement contained the essential contradiction of the rush: the men who had discovered the gold needed a body of other men to recognize that discovery, but every additional witness diminished their advantage. On June 6, they returned to Alder Creek.

The country Edgar had left only days earlier came back toward him beneath hooves, boots, and wagons. Men hurried along the creek looking for open ground. Stakes went in. Distances were measured. By the end of the day, the creek was claimed. The first institution arrived almost as quickly as the first crowd.

<!-- beat: claims-before-community -->

The miners organized the Fairweather District. A mining district was a jurisdiction created by the miners themselves, a practice carried from the California fields through each successive western rush. Where federal law offered no practical system for taking and holding mineral ground, the men within a defined area met, selected officers, recorded claims, and adopted rules governing ownership and work. The Fairweather District had a president, recorder, judge, and sheriff before there was a building worthy of any of them.

Gold required exactness so the rules were exact. They described how much ground a man might hold, how a claim could be acquired, and how far it extended across creek, bar, hill, gulch, or lode. They made absence consequential and possession legible. A stake driven into earth meant little unless surrounding men agreed to see a boundary there.

This was government, but government at the scale of immediate necessity. The miners had not come together because they had discovered a common political life. They had come because each wanted a portion of the same creek. Rules allowed them to work without fighting continuously over the ground. The authority of the district began in consent, proximity, and mutual advantage. Beyond that, authority thinned quickly.

There was no single community yet. There were agreements surrounded by claims. A miner might submit to a recorder who protected the dimensions of his claim and still see no reason to accept commands from an officer in another camp. A man who had crossed mountains, selected his own ground, built his own shelter, and fed himself did not necessarily consider distance a source of jurisdiction. Nevada City could be only a few miles away and still belong to other men with other rules.

<!-- beat: fourteen-mile-city -->

The work changed the gulch physically.

Edgar could measure the change without counting people. The banks where the discovery men had washed their pans disappeared beneath claims. A place they had tried to keep secret became one of the largest concentrations of newcomers in the northern Rockies. The creek remained where it had been, but almost nothing beside it did.

Alder and willow came down. Water was diverted through ditches and turned through sluices. Men cut into banks, shifted stones, stripped gravel from the old course of the creek, and piled tailings behind them. The sound of water joined axes, shovels, saws, animals, and the continuous striking of hammers.

Shelter followed labor. At first men lived under canvas, brush, overhanging rock, and whatever could be made before dark. Cabins appeared as timber could be cut and brought down. Streets formed where movement repeated itself. A bakery, saloons, stores, stables, and boardinghouses gave the tracks permanence.

Settlements acquired names along the length of the gulch: Summit, Highland, Pine Grove, Virginia City, Central City, Nevada City, Junction, Adobetown, and other names born and forgotten as camps appeared, merged, and vanished. Taken together, they became Fourteen-mile City. The name suggested unity more successfully than the ground did.

The gulch was long and narrow. Each camp occupied its own piece of the creek and looked toward its own immediate needs. Virginia City grew largest, but size did not make its reach certain. Between one cluster of cabins and the next lay distance enough for every vestige of authority to fade.

<!-- beat: borrowed-population -->

Probably none of the 1863 settlers had begun their western lives in Alder Gulch. This did not mean the country lacked a human past. Long before prospectors divided the creek into claims, Indigenous peoples had hunted, traveled, traded, and contested the valleys of southwestern Montana. Shoshone and Bannock communities had deep ties to the wider region; Crow and other nations also moved through it on their way between mountain country and the buffalo grounds. Their histories did not conform to the boundaries the miners would draw around districts, claims, and towns. The newcomers were imposing a new system of ownership on land whose older patterns of use they scarcely recognized.

They came first from Bannack and neighboring camps, then along the routes by which news and supplies traveled. Experienced miners carried techniques learned in California and through the successive strikes of Oregon, Nevada, Idaho, and the inland Northwest. Others came from Colorado, Salt Lake, the Missouri, and the states divided by war. Foreign-born men arrived with languages and distinctions the camps often compressed into rough national names.

They brought habits from other places. They knew how a rush moved and what happened when it arrived ahead of lumber, food, law, and winter preparation. Some understood placer ground. Some understood only that other men were finding gold. Many discovered that the claims worth having had already been marked. Mining therefore became only one way to live from the strike.

Every man working beside the creek required food, tools, boots, timber, candles, blankets, whiskey, horses, repairs, and a place to sleep. A merchant could profit from gold without touching a pan. A carpenter could build for men who would never own enough ground to employ him otherwise. A freighter could turn distance into a price. The gulch had been founded on extraction. Almost immediately, it required an economy capable of sustaining the people doing the extracting.

<!-- beat: sheehans-arrive -->

James Sheehan was a freighter. In a country where nearly every manufactured necessity arrived by wagon, the occupation carried more consequence than the title suggested. Miners might produce gold and merchants sell against it, but neither could remain in the gulch unless freighters kept flour, tools, clothing, and winter provisions moving over the roads. Reliability in that work conferred standing. Sheehan's work joined the physical demands of labor to the judgment and independence of business, and whole camps waited on men like him to arrive.

His family had followed western work through more than one town called Nevada and more than one country advertised as temporary. James had come from Ireland as a boy. He had worked in railroad construction, kept a store, and hauled provisions toward new settlements. By June 1863, the family had reached Bannack just as the news from Alder Creek escaped.

James took an early wagonload of freight to the new camp. Anne, his young wife, followed with his ten-year-old daughter Mary, their baby Kate, and James's niece Ellen.

Mary later remembered arriving in the wake of several hundred miners. The adults drove stakes because a boundary in dirt might contain a fortune. Mary sharpened a stick, wrote her name in the ground, and marked out a claim of her own.

The gesture amused her father. It also revealed how quickly the new logic had taken hold. A child understood that arrival conferred the right to draw a line and say the earth inside it belonged to her.

For Mary, the camps were not purely strange. She had already lived among cabins, mills, wagons, and the recurring disorder of western mining towns. She did not enter Alder Gulch knowing what it would become. Neither did the adults. She entered a place where everyone behaved as though the next act might make them rich.

<!-- beat: instant-economy -->

The Sheehans occupied a large log cabin on Wallace Street, the main thoroughfare through Virginia City. Because James hauled freight, the household possessed provisions scarce enough to be noticed. Anne and Ellen made dried apple and dried peach pies. In a camp accustomed to beans, meat, bread, and whatever a man could prepare for himself, the table drew business. They began taking boarders.

The household became part residence, part enterprise, and part public room. Men came in from claims and roads carrying mud, gold dust, news, and reputations that had not yet hardened. The discovery men ate there. Henry Edgar, who had seen the gulch before it acquired a street, could sit at a table in a cabin where none had existed weeks earlier.

Mary remembered Jack Gallagher stopping for meals. She remembered George Ives in his long blue soldier's coat, conspicuous among the men around the table. Later history would classify Gallagher and Ives as road agents. In the Sheehan cabin, before those classifications became death sentences, they were men eating food prepared by Anne and Ellen. That proximity was ordinary. It was also one of the defining conditions of the gulch.

There was little separation between domestic life and the population arriving around it. A boardinghouse could not screen every history at the door. A merchant sold to men he might later accuse. A child recognized a coat before she understood an allegation. Respectability and danger occupied the same rooms because there were not enough rooms to keep them apart.

James eventually closed the cabin to the public and moved the family into a smaller place off Wallace Street. Mary believed he had formed conclusions about some of the patrons. What he knew, and when he knew it, cannot be recovered cleanly from what the family learned afterward. The decision nonetheless marked a limit. Enterprise had drawn the public into the household. Concern sent it back out.

<!-- beat: local-rules-limited-consent -->

By then, the settlements possessed officers, courts, businesses, and rules. Their existence did not create an institution capable of standing behind all of them.

Miners' government worked best when the dispute was visible and the interested men were near enough to assemble. A recorder could preserve a claim. A jury could be called from the street. A judgment could be enforced when the surrounding crowd agreed that it should be.

The same arrangement was weak across distance and division. The gulch was too large to be one neighborhood and too new to possess settled allegiance. Officers held authority because men recognized it, and recognition could end when a decision became inconvenient. A sheriff without deputies, a judge without a jail, and a verdict without any durable force behind it remained dependent on the same population they purported to govern.

This did not mean that the inhabitants rejected order. They created order constantly. They simply created it for particular purposes, in particular places, and under conditions they could withdraw.

<!-- beat: beginnings-of-permanence -->

As summer became autumn, the camps acquired signs that extraction alone could not explain.

Families made households. Religious services gathered people who shared something other than a claim boundary. Children required occupation and instruction. Thomas Josiah Dimsdale, an educated Englishman who had come to Virginia City in poor health, opened a private school as winter closed around the gulch.

The school occupied a small cabin. Pupils brought an assortment of readers, arithmetic books, and copybooks because there was no system to supply them with the same ones. Mary Sheehan remembered Dimsdale as slight, gentle, and apparently in possession of all knowledge. He taught at a makeshift desk near a little window. The school did not prove that the camp had become permanent. It proved that people had begun behaving as though life continued beyond the next cleanup of a sluice.

Children whispered over lessons. Outside, men stripped gold from the creek and carried it through streets that had not existed in May. Dimsdale wrote during intervals between recitations. What he was writing then is uncertain. Within two years, he would use the territory's first newspaper to give the Vigilantes their earliest enduring history. For now he was a schoolteacher in a cabin, one more private citizen supplying a public need.

<!-- beat: december-present -->

By December, Alder Gulch could feed thousands of people without growing most of their food. It could house them without knowing how long they would remain. It could teach some of their children, hear disputes, record property, collect crowds, and create offices faster than distant government could fill them. Seven months had been enough to build a society in outline.

It was a society made from overlapping private arrangements. A merchant's credit, a freighter's schedule, a boardinghouse table, a mining district's rules, a teacher's tuition, a jury summoned from nearby men—each worked because the people immediately concerned agreed that it should. What the gulch could not do reliably was extend that agreement beyond sight.

Fourteen-mile City possessed no single force equal to its length. Between camps, authority diminished into distance. The people depended on roads for flour, tools, mail, newcomers, and news. Gold traveled out by the same routes. A freighter such as James Sheehan had to leave the household in order to sustain it. A miner who wished to convert dust into anything useful had to carry his wealth through open country. The roads joined the settlements. They also exposed everyone who used them. Everything the gulch needed came over those roads.

So did nearly everything worth stealing.
