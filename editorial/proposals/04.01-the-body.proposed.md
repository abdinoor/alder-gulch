# Chapter Four

## The Body

<!-- beat: nine-days -->

For nine days Nicholas Tiebolt did not return to Nevada City.

He had left to collect the span of mules he had sold to Butschy and Clark. The men had paid him before he went. When he failed to come back with the animals, his absence offered an explanation ready to hand: he had taken the money and kept the mules too.

It was an explanation his employers did not like. Tiebolt had worked for them long enough to earn their confidence. They knew him as sober and faithful, qualities that mattered because so much business in the camps depended upon property passing temporarily out of sight. A mule entrusted to another man went beyond the reach of a contract almost as soon as it left town. Payment did the same. Trust occupied the distance until the man returned.

Tiebolt had not returned.

The suspicion therefore rested uneasily beside what Butschy and Clark believed they knew of him. Each day made the suspicion easier to repeat and harder to reconcile with the man. There were no messages from the road, no regular patrols to question, and no office through which an overdue traveler became a missing person. A man could be delayed by weather, animals, drink, opportunity, or violence. Until someone found him, one absence looked much like another.

<!-- beat: palmer-hunts -->

William Palmer found Tiebolt because he shot a grouse.

Palmer was hunting in the Pas-sam-a-ri Valley, the open country settlers also called the Stinking Water. Away from the gulch, winter had reduced the land to muted colors: pale grass, dark sage, willow along the water, snow holding where the sun did not reach. A bird rising from cover briefly supplied the only urgent movement. Palmer fired. The report crossed the valley and thinned into distance.

He went to retrieve what he had shot.

The grouse had fallen in heavy sagebrush away from the road. Palmer pushed toward it, watching the ground for feathers. What first appeared among the brush did not resolve at once into a man. Cloth and frozen earth shared the same dull colors. An arm lay across the chest. The body was facedown, partly held by sage and willow, as though the vegetation had grown around it rather than received it nine or ten days before.

The grouse lay on or beside the body.

Palmer stopped. The small practical purpose that had brought him through the sage disappeared, but the bird remained where it had fallen. Near it was a coat, a hand, the shape of a head. The body did not belong to the road or to any shelter. It had been left where a traveler might pass without seeing it.

Cold had preserved what it had also made difficult to touch. Palmer moved close enough to see a wound above the left eye. Around the neck were marks narrow enough to suggest a small rope. The wrists, according to one account, were marked as well. One hand held willow; another account remembered pieces of sagebrush caught in both.

These things were visible. What they meant was not.

A gunshot wound could be named. So could the marks around the neck. Their order could not be read from the body by the men who found it. The brush did not say whether Tiebolt had grasped it or merely come to rest upon it. His position showed where he had been left, not how he had arrived there. No one present had the knowledge to determine how long he had lived after the wound, whether he had been conscious, or what had happened before the cold took hold of him.

Palmer knew only that the man was dead and that he could not leave him there.

<!-- beat: the-wakiup -->

There was a wakiup about a quarter of a mile away. Palmer went to it for help.

John Franck, called Long John, and George Hilderman were there. They lived close enough to the place that Palmer could reach them on foot, close enough that their presence would later acquire meanings it did not yet possess. Palmer told them what he had found and asked them to help put the body into his wagon.

They refused.

The accounts written afterward gave their refusal the hardness of a frontier verdict: men died every day; the body could remain where it was. Perhaps those were their words, or near enough to what they said that memory preserved the contempt. The exchange survived because later events made every act near Tiebolt's body appear consequential.

At the time, refusal proved only refusal. It did not tell Palmer whether the men had seen Tiebolt alive, heard a shot, recognized the body, or knew nothing at all. Indifference to a violent death could resemble concealment when the dead man later became important. It could also be indifference.

Palmer returned without them.

There were tasks in moving a body for which two additional men would have been useful. The limbs did not yield naturally. Clothing caught in the brush. The dead weight had to be worked free and raised into the wagon without the body's assistance. Palmer managed it alone, or without the help he had sought. The surviving accounts do not preserve the labor clearly. They preserve the refusal and the fact that the body reached Nevada City.

He placed Tiebolt in the wagon and turned toward the gulch.

<!-- beat: return-to-nevada -->

A wagon carrying a dead man traveled no faster because people were waiting for an answer.

The road brought Palmer back through the same country in which Tiebolt had disappeared. The body lay behind him, no longer hidden but not yet identified. Whatever violence it disclosed had already happened. Whatever danger remained could not be located. Palmer had found the result without finding its boundaries.

Nevada City supplied witnesses as soon as the wagon entered it. Men came close, then made room for others. Those who had heard of the missing hired man looked for the face they remembered. Those who had not known him looked at the wound. The difference mattered less in the first moments than it would later. Recognition and inquiry began in the same crowd.

The cold had altered Tiebolt but had not erased him. His clothes, age, and features offered one kind of identification. The possessions in his pockets offered another. The payment he had carried was not there. Neither were the mules he had gone to retrieve.

There was a knife.

William Herren carried word to Tom Baume. Of all the men who gathered around the body, Baume possessed a memory that reached beyond the gulch. About two years earlier, in Washington Gulch in the Colorado mining country, he had lent Tiebolt a knife. John X. Beidler and William Clark had been present. Men moving between camps borrowed objects because an object could be needed before a store appeared, and because no one wished to carry every useful thing he owned over every mountain. A knife passed from one man to another and remained with the borrower through country that left almost no other record of him.

Baume saw it again in Nevada City.

The knife did not explain the wound or the rope marks. It did not identify whoever had taken the gold and animals. It established something more immediate. The body Palmer had lifted from the sage was Nicholas Tiebolt.

The identification corrected the first story told about his absence. Tiebolt had not betrayed his employers and escaped with their property. Whatever had happened on the road, he had not returned because he had been killed.

<!-- beat: the-man-among-evidence -->

Once named, the body acquired relationships.

Butschy and Clark could no longer regard the missing gold and animals as a dishonest employee's choice. Baume could connect the dead man to another camp and another year. Clark had known him as an employer and, if the remembered loan was accurate, had known him in Colorado before either man came to Alder Gulch. The distance between those places contained most of Tiebolt's life in the West, and almost none of it survived in writing.

What remained could be held in a hand. A knife borrowed and kept. The clothes in which he had gone to fetch the mules. The reputation for fidelity that had made his disappearance seem wrong even when it appeared to accuse him. None was enough to recover his private hopes for the money or the path by which he had come from one mining country to the next. Together they prevented the injuries from becoming the whole of him.

Men still looked at the injuries.

The wound above the eye demanded an account. The marks around the neck suggested a rope. The marks at the wrists, if they were as Dimsdale later described them, suggested restraint. The brush caught in or beneath the hands could suggest movement after injury. Each observation invited the next conclusion. Soon men would say he had been dragged. Some would say he had been alive while it happened. The condition of the body made those possibilities difficult to dismiss.

It did not make them witnessed facts.

No physician's examination or coroner's record survives to separate what the body established from what observers believed it established. There was no secured ground to revisit. Palmer had moved Tiebolt because leaving him in the sage was intolerable, and in doing so had necessarily changed the arrangement in which he found him. Men examined the body in town after a wagon journey from the valley. They possessed signs of violence, an interrupted errand, absent property, and the conduct of people along the road. They did not yet possess the crime in sequence.

They began to ask who had last seen him.

<!-- beat: burial -->

Before the questions produced an accusation, the body had to be buried.

Elk Morse, William Clark, and Tom Baume arranged for a coffin. In a settlement built within months, even death required private men to supply what no institution reliably provided. Someone found boards. Someone measured the body. Nails were driven, the plain box assembled, and Tiebolt placed inside with the care available to men working in winter and without family known to be present.

They carried him above Nevada City.

From the burial ground, the cabins and workings lay below, close enough for sound to rise from them. Nevada City had been Tiebolt's probable working base, the place from which he departed and to which Palmer returned him. It was not home in any meaning the record can establish. No surviving account names a parent, sibling, wife, or child who learned where he had been put. The men at the grave were employers, acquaintances, and companions from the moving population of the camps. That did not make their obligation imaginary. It made them the people who were there.

The earth was winter earth. Whether they opened it with picks, used a place already prepared, or contended with frost in some other way was not recorded. The coffin went into the ground above the town. The knife had identified the man inside it; the knife's earlier owner stood near enough to see him buried.

No known marker preserves the place.

<!-- beat: public-fact -->

By the time the men came down from the grave, Tiebolt's disappearance had changed its nature.

There had been room, during the nine days, for delay, desertion, dishonesty, or accident. There was no longer room to believe he had chosen not to return. He had gone onto the road carrying payment and intending to collect two mules. He had been found concealed in brush away from that road, with a wound above the eye and marks around his neck. The money and animals were gone.

Those facts established violence. They did not yet establish its author.

The people of Nevada City possessed a body, several missing animals, absent gold, a hunting accident that had ended the search, two men who had refused to help, and a widening set of questions about who had been on the road. They could begin with the last known movements of the living. They could ask who had seen Tiebolt, who had seen the mules, and who had returned without him. They could compare what men said with what other men remembered.

But the body itself would answer no further questions. It had shown them what had been done to Nicholas Tiebolt only in fragments. The rest belonged to the living, and the living had not yet agreed on what they knew.
