# Chapter One

## The Chase

<!-- beat: landscape-and-frontier -->

Alder Gulch was fourteen miles of torn earth beneath an unbreaking sky.

The stream came down from the southern reaches of the Tobacco Root Mountains, through willows and alder, before surrendering itself to the Ruby Valley. In winter the hills stood bare, their shapes only hinted beneath a covering of deep snow. The mountains beyond them appeared blue in clear weather and vanished altogether when the weather came down. Between the mountains and the gulch lay vast distances that were at once beautiful and rough and empty.

The gulch was not empty. By December 1863, men had built settlements nearly end to end along it. Some were little more than names fastened to collections of tents and cabins. Others already had stores, saloons, boardinghouses, stables, and streets made permanent by mud. Virginia City was the largest. Nevada City lay just down-gulch to the northwest. Taken together, the camps were sometimes called Fourteen-mile City, as though giving the disorder a single name could make it one place.

Seven months earlier, none of it had existed. In May, six prospectors had stopped beside the creek and washed a pan of dirt. It panned out enough to stop prospecting and put down a stake. News crossed the mountains faster than government could follow, and men came up from Colorado, west from the Missouri, north from Salt Lake, and east from the exhausted fields of California and Oregon. They brought picks, sluice boxes, blankets, flour, whiskey, debts, and stories of the strike they had nearly made somewhere else. They stripped timber from the slopes and gravel from the creek. They built cabins where they intended to remain only until they were rich.

The richest placer strike yet found in the Rocky Mountains had appeared in a place that possessed almost none of the institutions wealth ordinarily summoned around itself.

The country was not yet Montana. Congress would not create Montana Territory until the following spring. Alder Gulch belonged, on paper, to Idaho Territory, whose government lay beyond the practical reach of the camps. The United States was two years into a civil war. Armies, money, and national attention were being consumed thousands of miles to the east. In the northern Rockies, jurisdiction could be easily drawn on a map but would be as valuable as the paper on which it was drawn.

The people of the gulch made such government as they required. They elected officers, organized miners' courts, and wrote rules for claims, water, and property. Those arrangements could settle a dispute when the interested men were near enough to assemble and willing to recognize the result. A man might submit his claim to a jury in the morning and carry a revolver to supper that night. The jury could give him a judgment. It could not accompany him home.

Gold moved through this country in buckskin sacks, bottles, folded paper, and the seams of clothing. It passed from creek bed to scale, from scale to counter, from counter to strongbox. Beyond the settlements it was commonly carried on horseback, in the possession of a man who could look across miles of open country and see no law between where he was and where he meant to go.

<!-- beat: tiebolt-in-flight -->

On one of those roads, Nicholas Tiebolt was riding hard.

At first there was only the sound. Hoofbeats crossed the frozen ground and traveled outward into the quiet. Then the horse came over a rise with its neck extended and its breath white before it. Tiebolt bent low over the saddle.

The animal was laboring. Its stride had begun to shorten, and the frost-dark hair beneath the saddle blanket had gone wet. Tiebolt urged it forward anyway. He looked back once, faced ahead, and looked back again before the horse had covered another hundred yards. Nothing showed above the rise. He rode on.

Tiebolt was young and foreign-born. Men in the camps called him Dutch, as they called many German-speaking immigrants, without troubling greatly over the distinction. He had been in the western mining country for at least two years.

Like most men who came west for gold, Tiebolt had found work. He worked for Butschy and Clark and had sold them a span of mules. They had paid him, and he had taken the payment to Dempsey's ranch to bring the animals back. The money was with him now.

It was not a fortune by the measure of Alder Gulch. Fortunes there were spoken of by the pan, the claim, and the wagonload. A smaller quantity could still hold a man's winter. It could buy food, tools, partnership in a claim, passage home, or another season in which to attempt what the last season had denied him. Tiebolt had earned the gold by parting with property. To collect the animals for their new owners, he had carried the gold back onto the road.

That much survived in the accounts. No witness left a record of the moment when the errand became a flight. No one recorded what Tiebolt first saw behind him or how long he waited before becoming afraid. There was only the country, the payment, the missing man, and the testimony that came afterward.

He looked back again.

A rider had appeared on the rise. The distance between them was great enough that the man had no face. Horse and rider formed one dark figure against the winter sky. The figure paused at the crest, then descended.

Tiebolt turned forward and drove his heels against his horse. The gold moved against him with the animal's stride. What had been payment now had another meaning, although its weight had not changed.

The road offered no cover. Sagebrush stood low against the wind. The willows followed the water and were too thin to conceal a mounted man. There was no occupied cabin in sight and no wagon approaching from the opposite direction. If Tiebolt called out, his voice would travel into the same open country as the hoofbeats.

The second horse was gaining. Its rider did not wave or call for Tiebolt to stop. He merely came on.

<!-- beat: ives-and-the-pursuit -->

George Ives was twenty-seven or thereabouts, nearly six feet tall, fair-haired and blue-eyed, with no whiskers. Men later remembered a light felt hat and a soldier's overcoat. The description mattered because witnesses would be asked where they had seen him, with whom, and in possession of what.

His name had been connected to violent men before Nicholas Tiebolt disappeared. He had been seen in the company of Henry Plummer, Buck Stinson, Ned Ray, and others whose names would later be assembled into something that looked like an organization. Whether they had formed the disciplined criminal band subsequently described—or whether fear, confession, association, and memory made the pattern afterward—would become one of the enduring questions of Alder Gulch.

There was less uncertainty about the fear Ives caused while he was alive. He had exchanged gunfire in the street. An early account accused him of obtaining money through menace and placed him in the robbery of a Salt Lake coach only weeks before Tiebolt vanished. Reputation could not prove a murder. It could still affect how men responded when they saw Ives coming.

Ives was alone on the road. The men later named as his confederates came together and separated as opportunity required. Whatever their connection to him, none was present now.

Tiebolt's horse stumbled and recovered. He gathered the reins, fighting the animal's head. The horse had begun to blow hard enough that he could hear it beneath the pursuit. Its hindquarters slipped on a patch of frozen ground. Tiebolt kept it upright. He stole a glance over his shoulder, careful not to throw off the horse's fragile balance again.

The light hat. The overcoat moving with the horse.

Tiebolt faced forward. There was no town before him, no officer, and no witness waiting beyond the next rise. The miners' courts of Alder Gulch could settle the ownership of a claim and summon a jury from the camps. Their authority could not cross this distance until someone survived to carry word of what had happened.

Tiebolt's horse was failing. Ives's was not.

The remaining ground disappeared quickly. First there had been a valley between them, then a long slope, then only the length of a field. Tiebolt urged the horse once more, but the animal had nothing left that fear could purchase.

Ives came alongside.

<!-- beat: incapacitation -->

What passed between them was afterward reconstructed from testimony, accusation, physical evidence, and the condition in which Tiebolt was found. The trial would hear of gunfire, a rope, the location of the wound, and the movement of the body. Men would argue over who had seen what, who had lied, and whether the evidence was sufficient to justify taking another man's life.

For now there were only two riders beyond the reach of town.

One horse veered. A hand caught at leather or cloth. Tiebolt lost a stirrup. The earth came up beneath him, hard with winter, and the sky turned once above the road.

The horses went on a short distance before slowing. One stood with its reins down, its sides working violently, steam lifting from its body. Tiebolt lay where he had fallen. Ives dismounted.

The gold was taken. The mules would not return with Tiebolt. In time, stories of what had happened on this road would separate, multiply, and harden. One man would be accused, then another. Witnesses would remember what they had seen and deny what they had not. The condition of Tiebolt's body would answer some questions and create others. What could not be recovered from him would have to be supplied by the living.

When the horses had gone, the country became quiet again. The sky remained immense. Snow held in the shadows. The road continued toward the settlements as though it had carried nothing more consequential than two men passing through.

There was no officer to secure the ground and no coroner coming. Nothing had yet been built that could transform what happened there into evidence, judgment, and punishment. Those things could begin only when another person arrived.

<!-- beat: closing -->

For the moment there was only Nicholas Tiebolt, alive but only just, in a country vast enough to conceal almost anything.

Before long, there would be a body. Then there would be a demand for justice.
