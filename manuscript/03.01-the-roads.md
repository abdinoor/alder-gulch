# Chapter Three

## The Roads

<!-- beat: sheehan-departure -->

One fall night, James Sheehan prepared to leave Virginia City carrying more gold than he could conceal on the belt beneath his clothes.

Someone had warned him that robbers knew about the shipment. He put the buckskin purses into an old carpetbag, laid his clothes over them, and buried the bag beneath his bedding and camp equipment in the wagon. The driver he hired did not know what lay under the load. James sent him ahead with instructions about where to camp and remained behind long enough to make it appear that the wagon had gone without the gold.

Late that night, he mounted a horse and rode after it alone.

The journey was necessary. Gold dust had to leave the gulch to purchase the flour, tools, clothing, and winter provisions that came back in the wagons. Remaining home would protect the money only by ending the business on which his household and customers depended.

Delay was part of a freighter's work. Snow, broken harness, lame animals, a stream running too high to ford, or a wagon sunk to its hubs could keep James beyond the expected day. Anne, Mary, Ellen, and the baby remained in Virginia City, unable to know whether some difficulty had delayed him or whether he was not coming home at all.

<!-- beat: southmayd -->

Late in November, Oliver's Salt Lake coach left Virginia City for Bannack with Thomas Caldwell driving. Leroy Southmayd, Captain Moore, and a discharged driver called Billy rode as passengers.

Near the point of departure, a man known as Old Tex stood watching the coach.

"I am sorry to see that rascal watching us," Moore said. "It bodes us no good."

"Oh, I think there's no danger," Southmayd said. "Robbery has played out. These fellows are beginning to understand that the people will hold them accountable for their villainies."

The judgment lasted only as long as the first miles.

At Lorrain's ranch, George Ives and Steve Marshland came up on horseback while Caldwell changed the coach team. They asked for fresh horses and were told none were available. Ives fed his mount and spoke of overtaking Tex at Cold Spring ranch. After the coach departed, the two riders passed it on the road.

The passengers continued to Point of Rocks and stayed until morning. Later, near Stone's ranch, three mounted men appeared ahead of them. From behind, their shotguns resting across their arms allowed them to resemble hunters for a little longer.

"They're queer-looking beings, Tom, anyhow," Southmayd said.

"They're road agents, Leroy. You may depend upon it."

"Well, I believe they are, but we can't help ourselves now."

The riders wheeled their horses and brought the guns to bear on the coach.

The men were masked. Southmayd and Caldwell nevertheless believed they recognized George Ives, Bill Graves, called Whiskey Bill, and Bob Zachary.

"Halt! Throw up your hands!"

Zachary dismounted to search them. Southmayd hesitated when he was told to climb down, watching for an opportunity to reach his revolver.

Ives moved the shotgun toward him. "If you do that again, I'll kill you."

Southmayd stepped down. Zachary removed his pistol and gold. When Billy tired and lowered his arms, the muzzles came back toward him and his hands went up again.

Caldwell had been entrusted with small sums belonging to other people, money he was meant to deliver or use for purchases. At gunpoint, the distinction between his property and another man's obligation offered no protection. The robbery reached through him toward people who were not on the coach and would learn of the loss only when the driver arrived without what they had trusted him to carry.

When the search was finished, the passengers were ordered aboard.

"Get up and skedaddle."

Southmayd asked Caldwell to drive slowly. He turned to study the riders as the coach moved away. Ives told him that if he looked back again, he would shoot the top of his head off. Southmayd faced forward, and the coach went on to Bannack.

People were waiting at the express office when it arrived late. A delayed coach gathered concern because everyone understood how many explanations the road could supply. Questions began before the passengers had climbed down.

Plummer was there as sheriff.

"Was the coach robbed today?" he asked.

Southmayd said it was and began to describe the three men.

"Be careful, Leroy," Judge Bissell said. "Very careful what you say to that man."

Southmayd named Ives, Graves, and Zachary.

"Leroy, your life isn't worth a cent," Bissell told him.

Another man said, "They'll kill you sure."

The warning revealed the second reach of the robbery. Gold could be taken on the road. A witness's freedom could be taken afterward.

Southmayd remained in Bannack for three days to finish his business. When he prepared to return, Buck Stinson and Ned Ray announced that they would travel on the same coach. Both were Plummer's deputies. Both were also men Southmayd and his friends feared.

The express agent urged him not to go.

"For God's sake, Leroy, don't go. These fellows mean to kill you."

"I've got to go. If you'll get me a double-barreled shotgun, I'll take my chances."

The agent supplied guns to Southmayd, Caldwell, and another passenger, a boy of about sixteen. Before they started, Southmayd told the boy that if trouble came he must shoot.

"You may be sure I'll do it, too, Southmayd," the boy said. "I'm not afraid of them."

The boy took the rear seat. Stinson and Ray sat facing him. Southmayd rode beside Caldwell and watched the two deputies behind them.

At the Stinking Water crossing, the coach encountered Zachary, Graves, and Aleck Carter, armed with guns, pistols, and knives. The riders followed it to Cold Spring station. During supper they drank and swaggered. When the coach started again, they rode ahead, then wheeled in the road and called for it to halt.

"Tom," Southmayd said, "I guess they've got us."

"That's so."

This time three guns rose from the coach. Southmayd aimed at Carter. Caldwell and the boy selected the other two men.

"We only want you to take a drink," Carter said.

A bottle passed between men holding one another at gunpoint. Southmayd and Caldwell barely touched it. The boy, warned by a movement of Southmayd's foot, did not drink.

The riders went ahead. Darkness approached as the coach continued toward Alder Gulch. The final road into Virginia City narrowed through country well suited to concealment.

"It's a rough night's work, Tom," Southmayd said, "but the worst is to come. If they attack us in the canyon, there is no possible chance for escape."

"They'll do it, sure," Caldwell said. "Let's leave the coach here and take to the brush."

There they would at least not remain fixed inside a vehicle on a known route.

Stinson told them they would not be attacked. When the coach moved again, he began singing loudly. Ray continued when Stinson tired. The singing lasted through the dangerous miles toward Virginia City.

The accounts written later treated the song as a signal calling off an ambush. Southmayd could not know that from his seat. He knew only that two armed deputies he distrusted were behind him, that armed men he believed had robbed him were somewhere on the road, that a sixteen-year-old boy held a shotgun across his knees, and that a voice kept sounding into the darkness until the lights of the settlement drew near.

The coach arrived without another attack. The next day, according to Southmayd's account, Ray warned him not to name the men he had recognized.

Nothing had been restored. The gold remained gone. The witness remained afraid. The sheriff's badge had not made it clear which men on the coach represented protection and which represented danger.

<!-- beat: holter -->

Anton Holter came north from Salt Lake City with wagons and emigrants during the last days of November. A mountain storm overtook the party on Blacktail Deer Creek. Holter and another man pushed their teams toward the Pas-sam-a-ri, struggling through water that rose midway up the wagon boxes, then waited for the weather to ease.

After moving the train to Ramshorn Creek, Holter took two yokes of oxen toward Virginia City to sell. He passed George Ives and another man on the road. The oxen did not sell. Holter started back toward camp intending to spend the night at Norris's ranch.

Near sundown, two riders approached him. Holter did not know Ives by sight. He nevertheless checked his pistol. Exposure had made suspicion general: an unknown rider was not a robber, but neither could he safely be presumed harmless. Holter found the pistol rusted badly enough that its cylinder would not turn.

He had little money. The failed sale seemed to offer its own protection.

"Where are you going?" one rider asked.

"Down to Norris's place. Do you know where he lives?"

"Yes." The rider studied him. "Have you got any money?"

"No. I'm dead broke."

"We'll see about that."

Holter offered his memorandum book. The man searched it.

"You've got money. Hand it over, or I'll shoot you."

"You're surely mistaken," Holter said. "I left what I had at camp, and had to borrow ten dollars in town."

The rider ordered him to turn out his pockets. A few forgotten greenbacks appeared. He took them.

Holter was told to move his cattle out of the road and not follow. As he turned the animals, he looked back and saw a pistol aimed at him.

The first shot passed through his hat and grazed his scalp. The second cap failed. Holter ran for an old beaver dam while the rider followed. A teamster hauling poles appeared on the road before another shot could be fired.

The presence of an ordinary working man saved Holter's life. There was no patrol to arrive, no alarm carried ahead, and no authority projected across the distance. There was only another traveler appearing at the necessary moment.

Holter reached safety with the cattle he had failed to sell. The robbers had obtained almost nothing. If his account was accurate, one of them had tried to kill him anyway. He had gone to Virginia City to sell two yokes of oxen and returned to camp with a furrow through his hair and a bullet hole in his hat.

<!-- beat: moody-caravan -->

In early December, Milton Moody started south from Virginia City with three wagons and an accompanying train of packers. John McCormick, William Sloan, John Rockfellow, John Bozeman, Henry Branson, Melanchthon Forbes, and M. V. Jones were among the men traveling with him. Between gold dust and treasury notes, the caravan carried a fortune large enough that later accounts placed it near eighty thousand dollars.

The amount made secrecy difficult. Men asked when a train would leave, which road it would take, who would travel with it, and what the wagons contained. Questions necessary to ordinary business could also prepare a robbery.

The party traveled through Blacktail Deer Canyon, Beaverhead Canyon, and Dry Creek country, a route with few settlers and long intervals in which no assistance could reach them. They did what men had begun to do when traveling with property: they went together and armed themselves.

At a camp in Blacktail Deer Canyon, two men approached through the brush after dark. The travelers did not see them. According to the later accounts, Dutch John Wagner and Steve Marshland came within a few yards of the fire with shotguns, examined the dispersed camp, and withdrew rather than attack.

The next morning, the men around the fire heard voices concealed by a turn in the road.

"You take my revolver and I'll take yours," one man said. "You come on right after me."

Guns came into hands around the fire. Revolvers were cocked. Wagner and Marshland rode into view with shotguns ready, then checked themselves when they saw the reception waiting for them.

"How do you do, Mr. Sloan?" Marshland said.

"Very well, thank you," Sloan answered.

The riders said they were looking for lost horses. No one asked why that search required exchanged revolvers and an approach through cover.

No one fired. The explanation was allowed to stand, and the riders continued past.

The caravan's safety depended upon behaving as though the explanation might be false. Later, when the packers rode ahead to choose a camp, the two men stopped the wagons on the divide between Red Rock and Junction. One held the drivers under his gun while the other searched them and the wagons.

They found little. The greater portion of the gold traveled with the packers ahead. When some of those men returned and challenged them, the robbery became a fight. Shots were exchanged. Both alleged robbers were wounded, and the caravan recovered what had been taken.

The travelers reached Salt Lake City. They had kept guns beside breakfast, read the posture of every approaching rider, divided the treasure between wagons and packers, and survived because enough men were prepared to shoot. At the next camp, the weapons remained within reach.

<!-- beat: tilden-and-departure -->

On November 13, Henry Tilden rode from Bannack toward Horse Prairie to gather livestock belonging to Sidney Edgerton and Wilbur Fisk Sanders.

Tilden had crossed the plains with the Edgerton household that year. He was young enough to belong within a family arrangement and old enough to be useful outside it. Ten or twelve miles on horseback to collect animals was the kind of errand from which a settlement was made. It required no hardened adventurer. It required a boy who could ride, find stock, and bring it home.

Night came before the work was finished. On his return, three armed men stopped him. Tilden carried little worth taking. According to the account he later gave, one of the men held a revolver at his head and warned that identifying them would cost him his life.

Tilden returned to Bannack wet and shaken after his horse fell with him while crossing the town ditch. He reported the robbery to the Edgerton household and said he believed he had recognized one of the men.

He named Henry Plummer, the sheriff.

The identification did not settle the matter. Sanders did not initially accept it. Plummer had been elected to protect Bannack and the surrounding district. A masked encounter in darkness, recalled by a frightened young man, could be mistaken. Yet Sanders also warned Tilden not to repeat the accusation. If the boy was wrong, silence would prevent a grave slander. If he was right, silence might keep him alive.

The same evening, Samuel Hauser and Nathaniel Langford were preparing to leave Bannack for Salt Lake City with a freighter. Friends already believed they were being watched and urged them to postpone the journey. Hauser and Langford concluded that waiting would not eliminate a danger they would face whenever they left. Langford loaded a double-barreled gun heavily. They carried cocked weapons in their hands through the twelve miles to Horse Prairie.

George Dart watched Langford force charge after charge into the barrels.

"What are you filling that gun so full of lead for?"

"If we have any trouble with the road agents," Langford said, "it will be tonight."

Their friends had believed them gone before Tilden returned. Had they known the travelers were still in town, Langford later wrote, they might have prevented their departure by force. Concern had acquired its own coercion. A man could be free to travel and still find the people who cared about him trying to keep him from the road.

Hauser and Langford reached Horse Prairie. At camp, small decisions took on the weight of defenses: where blankets were spread, whether a man slept beside a wagon or on open ground, whether a gun lay within reach, whether someone remained awake. They survived the journey.

Dimsdale and Langford later described Plummer and several alleged associates following them and considering an attack. Neither traveler witnessed such a council. What they knew at the time was that they were afraid, that Tilden had been stopped, and that their weapons remained in their hands for the portion of the road nearest home.

Fear did not require them to know whether every threat came from one organization. It required only the possibility that a stranger ahead knew what they carried and where they meant to sleep.

<!-- beat: peabody-caldwell -->

On October 26, a Peabody and Caldwell coach left Virginia City for Bannack.

The stage offered what solitary travel did not: a known route, a driver, passengers, scheduled stops, and ranches where horses could be changed. William Rumsey had taken the driver's place because the regular man was sick. Messrs. Mattison, Percival, and Wilkinson rode as passengers. Farther along, other travelers joined them.

Snow began after the coach crossed the hills near Virginia City and continued hard enough to slow the team. At John Baker's ranch on the Stinking Water, some of the relay horses could not be found. The delay cost two hours. Rumsey put together the best team available and drove hard for Dempsey's, then Point of Rocks, trying to recover the lost time.

At Bill Bunton's ranch on Rattlesnake, they were late enough that the next team had been turned loose. Men went to find the animals and returned without them. Darkness settled. The passengers stayed the night.

Nothing in the delay was extraordinary by itself. Horses strayed. Weather slowed a coach. A missed connection at one station created trouble at the next. The road depended upon people, animals, and information arriving where they were expected, and none of the three could be relied upon perfectly.

By morning, the coach had been given worn horses that could scarcely pull it faster than a walk. Bunton climbed aboard to help. The regular coaches caught up, and for a short time the concentration of passengers and vehicles suggested safety in numbers. Then the stages separated again. Rumsey's weakened team fell behind.

Two masked riders appeared with shotguns.

"Up with your hands."

The passengers raised them. Bunton jumped down and begged the men not to kill him. He said he had no weapon and offered himself for search. Rumsey remained on the box, holding the reins.

"Get down."

"You must be damned fools to think I'm going to get down and let this team run away," Rumsey said. "You don't want the team. It won't do you any good."

Bunton offered to hold the horses. Rumsey climbed down.

The robbers made him remove the passengers' weapons and place them on the ground. Then they made him collect their purses. The driver's familiarity with his passengers became a convenience for the men behind the guns. One victim disarmed another. One victim gathered the gold.

One passenger had come out of the mines with roughly two thousand dollars saved from his claim. He had divided the dust among buckskin purses and concealed them beneath his clothes, distributing the weight so it would not show. The arrangement survived the weather, the ranches, and the public coach. It did not survive a search at gunpoint.

The robbers took what they found. Before leaving, they warned the passengers that if any of them spoke about the robbery, they would come back and finish the matter.

The coach went on to Bannack. Its passengers had lost more than gold. The route had shown them each place at which ordinary difficulty could be used against them: a missing team, a night forced at a ranch, exhausted horses, a coach separated from company. Scheduled travel had not removed the danger of the road. It had made the travelers' movements easier to learn.

<!-- beat: magruder -->

In October, Lloyd Magruder closed his store on Wallace Street and prepared to return home.

He had come from Lewiston that summer with a pack train loaded for the new mining camp. The merchandise had been selected for men who had arrived before the ordinary means of supporting them: clothing, tools, provisions, and whatever else could survive the journey and command the prices distance permitted. By autumn, most of it had been sold. Magruder carried the proceeds with him, commonly reported as about twelve thousand dollars in gold—roughly six hundred ounces of gold dust.

Four men traveled in his party: Charles Allen, Horace and Robert Chalmers, and William Phillips. Magruder also needed help managing the animals and guarding the return journey through the mountains. Four additional men offered their services. He gave them passage and better mules to ride, turning their poorer animals into the herd.

Allen distrusted them. He warned Magruder that the men might mean to rob him. Whatever concern the warning produced, it did not prevent the party from leaving. To turn back now would require him to admit that he feared the men accompanying him more than the journey ahead.

Beyond the Clearwater River, the party stopped and released the animals to graze. Magruder went up the slope with Bill Lowry to watch the herd. The other four intended victims remained below.

The account established afterward came principally through the prosecution of the surviving killers. Lowry murdered Magruder on the slope. Jem Romaine, Doc Howard, and William Page attacked the men in camp, killing Allen, the Chalmers brothers, and Phillips before they could organize a defense. The bodies, baggage, and weapons were rolled together and thrown over a precipice.

The killers continued toward Lewiston with the gold and the party's animals. They avoided Elk City, but they could not arrive home without bringing evidence of the missing men with them. Magruder's mule, saddle, leggings, and other possessions were recognized. The gold that had made the journey worth killing for made an ordinary return difficult to explain.

Deputy Marshal Hill Beachy followed the suspects to California, arrested them, and brought them back to Lewiston. They were tried and convicted. When men returned to the murder site the following spring, they found a few bones, buttons from Magruder's coat, and some of the party's firearms.

The people of Virginia City had watched Magruder open a store, supply their needs, and leave with men hired to protect him. Months later, what remained of him was identified by the buttons from his coat.

<!-- beat: sheehan-ruse -->

James Sheehan overtook his wagon without being challenged and completed the journey without being robbed.

Years later, Mary believed her father had been spared at George Ives's direction because Anne and the girls lived in the gulch. She had the story through later confessions and family recollection. It could not establish who watched the wagon, whether anyone had planned an attack, or why none occurred.

What Mary remembered without qualification was the waiting. She remembered the days and nights through which the family did not know whether her father was delayed, robbed, injured, or dead.

The people of Alder Gulch did not experience the roads as a later historian's list of crimes. Anne Sheehan waited for a husband. Mary and the other children waited for their father. A merchant wondered whether his goods or his payment would arrive. A passenger studied the men beside him. A witness who named a robber had to decide whether he could safely go home. A boy carried a shotgun because the adults believed the men sharing his coach might try to kill them.

Dimsdale would gather these incidents into the work of a single road-agent band commanded by Sheriff Henry Plummer. Later writers would repeat the organization, its officers, spies, signals, and system for marking travelers. The pattern answered a need beyond historical explanation. It made scattered fear legible. If the violence possessed a hierarchy, the community could imagine removing its head.

Some of the robberies were witnessed. Some assailants were recognized or later confessed. The Magruder killers were tried and convicted. The passengers on the coaches knew that their gold was gone. Tilden knew that a pistol had been held against him. Holter carried a hole through his hat. These facts did not depend upon the existence of Plummer's organization. Neither did the fear.

By December, the roads still carried flour, mail, animals, passengers, and gold. They still joined the settlements to one another and the gulch to the wider country. But every journey now required private precautions against a public danger: concealment, companions, weapons, secrecy, speed, or luck.

Danger found the men who had come west expecting it. It also found the households and communities that depended upon their return.
