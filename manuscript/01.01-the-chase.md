# Chapter One

## The Chase

<!-- beat: landscape-and-frontier -->

Alder Gulch was fourteen miles of torn earth beneath an unbreaking sky.

The stream came down from the southern reaches of the Tobacco Root Mountains, through willows and alder, before surrendering itself to the Ruby Valley. In winter the hills stood bare, their shapes only hinted beneath a covering of deep snow. The mountains beyond them appeared blue in clear weather and vanished altogether when the weather came down. Between the mountains and the gulch lay vast distances that were at once beautiful and rough and empty.

The gulch was not empty. By December 1863, men had built settlements nearly end to end along it. Some were little more than names fastened to collections of tents and cabins. Others already had stores, saloons, boardinghouses, stables, and streets made permanent by mud. Virginia City was the largest. Nevada City lay just down-gulch to the northwest. Taken together, the camps were sometimes called Fourteen-mile City, as though giving the disorder a single name could make it one place.

Seven months earlier, none of it had existed.

In May, six prospectors had stopped beside the creek and washed a pan of dirt. It panned out enough to stop prospecting and put down a stake. News crossed the mountains faster than government could follow, and men came up from Colorado, west from the Missouri, north from Salt Lake, and east from the exhausted fields of California and Oregon. They came with picks, sluice boxes, blankets, flour, whiskey, debts, and stories of the strike they had nearly made somewhere else. They stripped timber from the slopes and gravel from the creek. They built cabins where they intended to remain only until they were rich.

The richest placer strike yet found in the Rocky Mountains had appeared in a place that possessed almost none of the institutions wealth ordinarily summoned around itself.

The country was not yet Montana. Congress would not create Montana Territory until the following spring. Alder Gulch belonged, on paper, to Idaho Territory, whose government lay far beyond the practical reach of the camps. The United States was two years into a civil war. Armies and money and national attention were being consumed thousands of miles to the east. In the northern Rockies, jurisdiction could be easily drawn on a map but would be as valuable as the paper on which it was drawn.

The people of the gulch therefore made such government as they required. They elected officers. They organized miners’ courts. They wrote rules for claims and water and property. A man might submit a dispute to a jury in the morning and carry a revolver to supper that night because the jury’s authority ended where another man ceased to respect it.

The frontier was not the absence of people. It was the absence of anything certain behind them.

Gold moved through this country in buckskin sacks, in bottles, in folded paper, and in the seams of clothing. It passed from creek bed to scale, from scale to counter, from counter to strongbox. It was carried on horseback because there were few other ways to carry it. Every ounce had weight. Every road had distance. A man with gold upon him could look across miles of open country and understand that no law stood between where he was and where he meant to go.

<!-- beat: tiebolt-in-flight -->

On one of those roads, Nicholas Tiebolt was riding hard.

At first there was only the sound. Hoofbeats crossed the frozen ground and traveled outward into the quiet. Then the horse came over a rise with its neck extended and its breath white before it.

Tiebolt bent low over the saddle.

The animal was laboring. Its stride had begun to shorten, and the frost-dark hair beneath the saddle blanket had gone wet. Tiebolt urged it forward anyway. He looked back once, faced ahead, and looked back again before the horse had covered another hundred yards.

Nothing showed above the rise.

He rode on.

Tiebolt was young and foreign-born. Men in the camps called him Dutch, as they called many German-speaking immigrants, without troubling greatly over the distinction. He had been in the western mining country for at least two years. Long enough to learn that most men who came for gold found work instead. Long enough to understand that wages earned beside a rich claim were not the same thing as owning one.

He worked for Butschy and Clark. He had sold them a span of mules, and they had paid him. The money was with him now.

It was not a fortune by the measure of Alder Gulch. Fortunes there were spoken of by the pan, the claim, the wagonload. But a small quantity of gold could contain an entire future when it belonged to one man. It could buy food through winter. It could purchase tools, partnership in a claim, passage home, or another season in which to attempt what the last season had denied him.

Tiebolt had taken the gold and ridden to Dempsey’s ranch to bring back the animals.

That much survived in the accounts.

No witness left a record of the moment when the errand became a flight. No one recorded what Tiebolt first saw behind him or how long he waited before becoming afraid. There was only the country, the money, the missing man, and the testimony that came afterward.

He looked back a third time.

A rider had appeared on the rise.

The distance between them was great enough that the man had no face. Horse and rider formed one dark figure against the winter sky. The figure paused at the crest, then descended.

Tiebolt turned forward and drove his heels against his horse.

The gold moved against him with the animal’s stride. What had been payment now had another meaning. Its weight had not changed. Everything around it had.

The road offered no cover. Sagebrush stood low against the wind. The willows followed the water and were too thin to conceal a mounted man. There was no settlement in sight, no occupied cabin, no wagon approaching from the opposite direction. If Tiebolt called out, his voice would travel into the same open country as the hoofbeats.

He looked back again.

The second horse was gaining.

Its rider sat easily despite the speed. He did not wave or call for Tiebolt to stop. He merely came on.

<!-- beat: ives-and-the-pursuit -->

George Ives was twenty-seven or thereabouts, nearly six feet tall, fair-haired and blue-eyed, with no whiskers. Men remembered the light felt hat and the soldier’s overcoat. They remembered him as handsome until they had reason to examine him more closely.

There were already reasons.

His name had been connected to violent men before Nicholas Tiebolt disappeared. He had been seen in the company of Henry Plummer, Buck Stinson, Ned Ray, and others whose names would later be assembled into something that looked like an organization. Whether they had truly formed the disciplined criminal band subsequently described—or whether fear, confession, association, and memory made the pattern afterward—would become one of the enduring questions of Alder Gulch.

There was less uncertainty about the danger of Ives himself.

He had exchanged gunfire in the street. He had been accused of using menace as casually as another man used credit. An early account would place him in the robbery of a Salt Lake coach only weeks before Tiebolt vanished. Men who crossed him had learned that his good appearance did not make him safe.

He was alone on the road now.

But a man could ride alone and still carry the strength of other men with him. Reputation traveled ahead. Association remained when companions were out of sight. The possibility of unseen allies could make one rider seem like several.

Tiebolt’s horse stumbled and recovered.

He gathered the reins, fighting the animal’s head. The horse had begun to blow hard enough that he could hear it beneath the pursuit. Its hindquarters slipped on a patch of frozen ground. Tiebolt kept it upright. He stole a glance over his shoulder, careful not to throw off the horse’s fragile balance again.

Ives was close enough now to be recognized.

The light hat. The overcoat moving with the horse. The unhurried seat.

Tiebolt faced forward. There was no town before him. No sheriff. No magistrate. No witness waiting beyond the next rise. The miners’ courts of Alder Gulch could settle the ownership of a claim and summon a jury from the street, but they could do nothing for a man alone between settlements. Their power could not cross the distance until someone survived to carry word.

Tiebolt’s horse was failing.

Ives’s was not.

The remaining ground disappeared quickly. First there had been a valley between them, then a long slope, then only the length of a field. Tiebolt urged the horse once more, but the animal had nothing left that fear could purchase.

Ives came alongside.

<!-- beat: incapacitation -->

What passed between them was afterward reconstructed from testimony, accusation, physical evidence, and the condition in which Tiebolt was found. The trial would hear of gunfire. It would hear of a rope. It would hear where the wound entered and how the body had been moved. Men would argue over who had seen what, who had lied, and which part of the story could justify taking another man’s life.

For now there were only two riders beyond the reach of town.

One horse veered.

A hand caught at leather or cloth. Tiebolt lost a stirrup. The earth came up beneath him, hard with winter, and the sky turned once above the road.

Then the movement stopped.

The horses went on a short distance before slowing. One stood with its reins down. Its sides worked violently. Steam lifted from its body.

Tiebolt lay where he had fallen.

Ives dismounted.

Nothing in sight represented authority except the strength of the man still standing.

The gold was taken. The mules would not return with Tiebolt. In time, Ives would ride among other men and the stories of what had happened on this road would begin to separate, multiply, and harden. One man would be accused, then another. Witnesses would remember what they had seen and deny what they had not. A dead man would be asked to bear more certainty than the living could supply.

Tiebolt did not get away.

When the horses had gone, the country became quiet again. The sky remained immense. Snow held in the shadows. The road continued toward the settlements as though it had carried nothing more consequential than two men passing through.

There was no officer to secure the ground. No coroner was coming. No institution had yet been built that could transform what happened there into evidence, judgment, and punishment.

<!-- beat: closing -->

For the moment there was only Nicholas Tiebolt, alive but only just, in a country vast enough to conceal almost anything.

Before long, there would be a body.

Then there would be a demand for justice.
