# Chapter Four

## The Body

<!-- beat: nine-days -->

Nicholas Tiebolt had gone to Dempsey's ranch for the mules he had sold Butschy and Clark, taking with him the money they had paid. Nine days later, he was still not back. With each passing day, the trust behind that ordinary errand looked more and more misplaced.

Butschy and Clark had known him as sober and faithful. He had worked for them long enough that they were willing to place money in his hands and let the property pass out of sight. The arrangement put both payment and animals beyond their immediate control. They had paid Tiebolt, and now both he and the mules were gone.

His reputation made them reluctant to conclude that he had stolen from them, but the days supplied no other explanation. There was no office where they could report an overdue man and expect someone else to begin looking. Weather, accident, drink, opportunity, and violence could all delay a traveler; from Nevada City, each began with the same absence.

<!-- beat: palmer-hunts -->

William Palmer found Tiebolt because he shot a grouse.

He was hunting in the Pas-sam-a-ri Valley, the open country settlers also called the Stinking Water. Pale grass showed between patches of snow and dark sage, while willow followed the water toward hills bare enough to expose anything crossing them. A grouse rose from cover. Palmer fired, watched it fall into heavy brush away from the road, and went after it.

Looking for feathers among the branches, he first saw cloth close to the color of the frozen ground. An arm lay across a chest. A head showed beyond it. The man was facedown, caught among sage and willow, with the grouse on or beside him.

Palmer bent over the body and turned the head far enough to bring the face into view. He could not identify the man. There was a wound above the left eye. Narrow marks encircled the neck, as if from a small rope, and Dimsdale would later report similar marks at the wrists. Vegetation remained caught in the man's hands: willow in one account, broken sagebrush in another. To Palmer, it looked less as though the brush had fallen there than as though the man had seized it and held fast. Whether that grip belonged to his final living moments or the vegetation had gathered beneath him when he fell, Palmer could not know. Nor could he tell whether the wound or the rope marks had come first. The body had been there long enough to freeze.

He left him in the sage and walked to a wakiup about a quarter of a mile away to ask for help.

<!-- beat: the-wakiup -->

John Franck, known as Long John, and George Hilderman were there. Palmer told them that a dead man lay in the sage and asked them to help put him in the wagon. They refused. The later accounts preserved their answer as an expression of indifference: men met violent deaths every day, and there was no reason for them to trouble themselves over this one.

Their refusal would later be treated as suspicious because they lived near the body and would be questioned about what they had seen. At the time, it established only that they would not assist Palmer. He returned to the sage without them.

The accounts do not say how he moved the frozen body through the brush or raised it into the wagon. They record that he managed to transport it to Nevada City while Long John and Hilderman remained at the wakiup.

<!-- beat: return-to-nevada -->

The journey back followed the same roads on which Tiebolt had disappeared. Palmer knew the man had been shot and had marks around his neck, but he did not know where the violence had begun or who else had been present.

People gathered when the wagon reached Nevada City. Men who had heard of the missing hired hand searched the face for someone they knew; others looked first at the wound. Cold had altered the features enough to make identification uncertain, so they examined the clothing and the contents of the pockets. The money Tiebolt had carried was gone. The mules were not with the body.

There was a knife.

William Herren went for Tom Baume. When Baume came to the wagon, someone showed him the knife from the dead man's pocket. About two years earlier, in Washington Gulch in the Colorado mining country, Baume had lent that knife to Nicholas Tiebolt in the presence of John X. Beidler and a man named William Clark.

The knife had remained with Tiebolt through the unrecorded part of his journey from Colorado to Alder Gulch. Baume's recognition identified the body and ended the suspicion that Tiebolt had taken the payment and kept the mules. He had failed to return because he was dead.

<!-- beat: the-man-among-evidence -->

Butschy and Clark now knew that the employee they had suspected of stealing from them had been killed. Baume could place Tiebolt in Washington Gulch two years earlier, although the record says little about their relationship beyond the loan. The William Clark who reportedly witnessed it may have been the same Clark who later employed Tiebolt, but the surviving sources do not establish that identity.

The knife, Tiebolt's clothing, and his employers' memory of his fidelity and sobriety supplied most of what the men in Nevada City could recover about him. They did not know what he had intended to do with the payment or where he had lived between Washington Gulch and Nevada City. Even his precise birthplace remained unknown.

They knew more about the condition of his body. The wound above the eye, the narrow marks at the neck, the reported marks at the wrists, and the vegetation caught in his hands suggested restraint and movement after injury. Men would later say that Tiebolt had been dragged through the brush while still alive. No one examining him in Nevada City had witnessed that sequence, and no physician's report or coroner's record survives to establish it.

<!-- beat: burial -->

Elk Morse, William Clark, and Tom Baume procured a coffin and buried Tiebolt above Nevada City. No family member appears in the surviving account. The men present knew him through work or an earlier mining camp, and the sources preserve neither the words spoken at the burial nor the time required to open the winter ground.

Nevada City had probably been Tiebolt's working base, but the record does not establish whether he considered it home. No parent, sibling, wife, or child is known to have learned of his death. The grave was somewhere above the town; its exact location and survival are unknown.

<!-- beat: public-fact -->

Tiebolt's absence was no longer a question of delay, desertion, or dishonesty. He had left Nevada City carrying payment and intending to bring back two mules. Palmer had found him in heavy brush away from the road, wounded above the eye and marked at the neck. The payment and animals were gone.

The people of Nevada City knew that he had been killed, but the condition of the body did not establish the sequence or identify whoever had taken part. They needed to determine who had last seen Tiebolt alive, who had seen the mules, and what the men living near the discovery site knew. Citizens returned to the vicinity of the wakiup and began questioning Long John.
