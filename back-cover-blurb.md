# *Alder Gulch* — Back-Cover Blurb

In December 1863, the people of Nevada City, a remote gold camp in Montana’s Alder Gulch, put George Ives on trial for the murder of Nicholas Tiebolt. They had no courthouse, so they made one in the street. A wagon became the judge’s bench. The jury gathered around a fire against the cold. Lawyers and witnesses stood on a soapbox while hundreds of armed men watched.

The proceedings would be recognizable today. Ives had defenders. Witnesses were examined. Evidence was contested. For three days, an isolated mining community submitted itself to the forms of law—and to the possibility that a jury might set a feared man free.

History remembers Ives as the road agent whose conviction and hanging inspired the Montana Vigilantes. *Alder Gulch* tells the larger and more unsettling story. While one form of justice was being constructed in public, another was taking shape in private. The trial of a single man had exposed a terrible question: What do frightened people owe to justice when justice cannot keep them safe?
