# George Ives: Factual Background

## The setting

In 1863, Alder Gulch was part of Idaho Territory; Montana Territory would not be created until 1864. The discovery of gold had produced an enormous population boom around Virginia City and Nevada City, but essentially no functioning territorial judicial system. Local communities therefore created miners' courts to settle disputes and try crimes.

At the same time, travelers carrying gold were being robbed and sometimes murdered by “road agents.” George Ives had acquired a reputation as one of these men and was associated with several suspected criminals around Alder Gulch. Later accounts connected him with the supposed gang allegedly headed by Bannack sheriff Henry Plummer, although historians have questioned how organized that “gang” actually was.

## The murder accusation

Ives was accused of murdering Nicholas Tiebolt—whose surname appears in historical sources under several spellings, including Tbalt and Thiebalt—a young immigrant who had gone to retrieve livestock.

The prosecution's case was essentially that Ives had killed Tiebolt and taken his property. Ives was arrested and brought to Nevada City for trial.

## The improvised Nevada City trial

The trial began around December 19, 1863, and continued for roughly three days.

There was no courthouse. Proceedings took place on Nevada City's main street in bitter winter weather. Dr. Don Byam acted as judge. The jury reportedly sat around a large log fire, with Byam presiding from a wagon. Contemporary and reconstructed accounts put the crowd as high as 2,000 people, an enormous gathering given the frontier setting.

The prosecutor was Wilbur Fisk Sanders, a young lawyer who later became a major Montana political figure.

The proceeding was surprisingly elaborate for a frontier miners' court. Witnesses were examined, arguments were made, Ives had defenders, and a jury considered the evidence. But the atmosphere was hardly neutral: Ives had armed friends present, Sanders's supporters were armed as well, and there was a genuine possibility that the proceedings could turn into a gunfight.

## Verdict and execution

The jury found Ives guilty of murder.

There was effectively no appellate process, incarceration system, or mechanism for carrying out a sentence weeks or months later. Ives attempted to obtain a delay before execution. According to one famous account, when the question arose of giving him additional time, X. Beidler shouted:

> “Sanders, ask him how long he gave the Dutchman.”

The demand for delay collapsed.

On December 21, 1863, Ives was hanged in Nevada City, essentially immediately following his conviction.
