# Writing a Novel in the *In Cold Blood* Tradition

To write in the *In Cold Blood* tradition, approach a real event with a reporter’s discipline and a novelist’s sense of structure. The crucial rule: use fiction’s techniques, but don’t invent its facts.

Start with a case or event that opens onto a larger subject—class, violence, justice, community, ambition, or grief—not merely something sensational.

## 1. Research Before Interpreting

Collect interviews, records, photographs, correspondence, timelines, court documents, and contemporary reporting. For every important detail, record its source and confidence level.

Separate your notes into:

- Verified fact
- Source’s recollection
- Disputed claim
- Your inference
- Unknown

Never quietly turn the last three into the first.

## 2. Choose Several Narrative Threads

Instead of following one person chronologically, select perspectives that reveal different sides of the event—for example:

- The people most directly affected
- The person responsible
- An investigator or institutional figure
- The surrounding community

Alternate among them until their stories converge. End sections at moments that create questions or unsettling contrasts.

## 3. Create Dramatic Irony

Capote tells readers early that catastrophe is coming. Ordinary actions therefore carry enormous weight. You can use the same principle:

> While one character prepares for a normal evening, another travels toward the event that will end it.

The tension comes from the reader knowing more than the people on the page.

## 4. Write Scenes From Evidence

Treat every scene as a small dramatic unit:

- Establish where and when it occurs.
- Give someone an immediate objective.
- Introduce resistance or uncertainty.
- Use a few revealing physical details.
- End with a change, decision, discovery, or implication.

Only describe thoughts when they were expressed in an interview, letter, diary, recording, or other reliable source. Otherwise, describe observable behavior and preserve the uncertainty: “She later recalled…” or “He appeared…”

## 5. Delay the Central Explanation

You need not reveal every detail at the moment the event occurs. A strong structure might be:

- **Part I — Before:** Parallel lives moving toward the event
- **Part II — Absence:** Aftermath, confusion, and investigation
- **Part III — Answer:** Discovery, arrest, and reconstruction
- **Part IV — Judgment:** Trial, consequences, and unresolved meaning

Withholding should organize verified information—not manufacture false suspense.

## 6. Develop People Through Contradiction

Avoid saints, monsters, and case-file summaries. Look for opposing qualities: tenderness beside cruelty, confidence beside insecurity, public reputation beside private behavior. Include context without pretending that explanation equals excuse.

## 7. Keep the Narration Controlled

Use clear, concrete prose and let juxtaposition carry your judgment. Recurring images or objects can unify the book, but resist melodrama. Specificity is stronger than adjectives:

> The porch light remained on after sunrise.

That often lands harder than telling readers a house felt tragic.

## 8. Address the Ethics Openly

Obtain informed consent where appropriate, protect vulnerable people, corroborate damaging allegations, and offer relevant subjects a fair chance to respond. Keep recordings and citations. Consider an author’s note explaining your methods, reconstructed material, uncertainties, pseudonyms, and composite characters—ideally, avoid composites entirely.

For allegations involving living people, publication also warrants professional fact-checking and legal review.

## Drafting Formula

**Documented fact → concrete scene → conflicting perspective → larger implication**

Your goal is not to imitate Capote’s voice. It is to reproduce the book’s central engine: parallel lives, an inevitable convergence, meticulous detail, psychological complexity, and tension between private experience and public judgment.

## A Second Inspiration: *True Grit*

Where *In Cold Blood* offers structure, documentary discipline, and shifting perspectives, *True Grit* offers a commanding narrator and a morally charged journey. The two influences can work together if each has a distinct job.

Draw from *True Grit* through:

- **A strong retrospective voice:** Let the narrator tell the story from a later vantage point, with firm opinions about what happened and what it meant.
- **A concrete objective:** Build the narrative around a difficult pursuit that can be stated plainly. The deeper themes should emerge from the pursuit rather than replace it.
- **Determined characterization:** Give the central figure a code, an appetite for action, and qualities that are both admirable and limiting.
- **Plainspoken authority:** Favor exact nouns, active verbs, dry observation, and controlled wit over ornate explanation.
- **Moral friction:** Put characters with incompatible ideas of justice, duty, courage, and mercy into situations where they must depend on one another.
- **An ordeal that changes the meaning of the quest:** The external goal may be achieved, but its human cost should complicate any simple idea of victory.
- **A vivid social world:** Use speech, work, money, law, religion, landscape, and local custom to make the setting exert pressure on every decision.

### Combining the Two Models

A useful hybrid design would be:

1. Open with a distinctive narrator announcing the event and why it still matters.
2. Establish the people and community through carefully documented scenes.
3. Launch a clear pursuit—of a person, an answer, justice, or restitution.
4. Alternate that journey with other perspectives that complicate the narrator’s certainty.
5. Let the threads converge in an ordeal or confrontation.
6. End from the retrospective vantage point, showing what time has clarified and what remains unresolved.

The resulting book could combine **Capote’s converging factual architecture** with **Portis’s forceful narrative personality**. Preserve the distinction between them: the evidence determines what happened, while the narrator’s voice shapes how the experience is understood.

## A Third Inspiration: *The Devil in the White City*

Erik Larson’s *The Devil in the White City* adds another useful model: braided historical narrative. It places an extraordinary public achievement beside a hidden sequence of crimes, allowing each story to darken and intensify the other.

Draw from it through:

- **Two contrasting narrative lines:** Pair a visible project—building, reforming, exploring, governing, creating—with a concealed or destructive undertaking unfolding nearby.
- **A consequential historical setting:** Treat the period as an active force. Technology, politics, economics, social customs, and physical geography should create opportunities and constraints.
- **A cast organized around central figures:** Give each narrative line a compelling human anchor, then introduce supporting characters according to their relationship to that person’s objective.
- **Alternation with purpose:** Switch threads at moments of decision, danger, revelation, or strong thematic contrast. Each transition should make readers reconsider the scene they just left.
- **Escalating logistics:** Show how ambitious undertakings are made through deadlines, money, labor, rivalry, improvisation, and failure. Practical obstacles can generate as much tension as overt danger.
- **Selective exposition:** Introduce historical context when it changes the meaning of an immediate choice. Avoid stopping the story for a detached lecture.
- **Documented suspense:** Arrange facts to preserve uncertainty experienced by people at the time, while remaining honest about what the evidence can establish.
- **Convergence through place and time:** Even if the central figures never meaningfully meet, their stories can become connected by the same city, institution, event, or historical moment.

### A Three-Influence Design

The three models can serve different functions:

- ***In Cold Blood* — investigative architecture:** parallel perspectives, psychological depth, accumulating evidence, and inevitable convergence.
- ***True Grit* — narrative personality:** a memorable retrospective voice, a concrete quest, moral conviction, and the cost of pursuing justice.
- ***The Devil in the White City* — historical scale:** contrasting storylines, an ambitious public undertaking, period detail, and a setting that unifies otherwise separate lives.

A possible structure would be:

1. **The Promise:** Introduce the narrator, the place, the great undertaking, and the approaching threat.
2. **The Pursuit:** Follow the central quest while alternating between the public project and the concealed story.
3. **The City Transformed:** Increase the scale, compress the timeline, and show private choices colliding with historical forces.
4. **The Reckoning:** Bring the investigation or pursuit to its answer and reveal the human cost of the celebrated achievement.
5. **The Memory:** Return to the retrospective voice to distinguish what the place remembers from what the evidence reveals.

The strongest synthesis is not a collage of three authors’ styles. It is a division of labor: **Capote supplies the tension, Portis supplies the voice, and Larson supplies the historical canvas.**
